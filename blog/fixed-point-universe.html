Can a Universe Explain Its Own Existence?
A Fixed-Point Proposal

A research note on formalizing self-consistent worlds.

June 2, 2026 12 min read Navid Mirnouri

The question

Start with the oldest question there is: why does anything exist? Every answer seems to need something prior — a cause, a creator, a law — and then that prior thing needs its own explanation. The chain either runs forever, stops at something unexplained (a "brute fact"), or loops back on itself.

This note is about taking the third option seriously and trying to make it precise.

The intuition: imagine reality as a structure that produces itself. Not a chain with a first link, but a loop — where the "beginning" is entailed by what it gives rise to, and the whole thing hangs together with no input from outside. If such a structure is coherent, it would need nothing external. It would be self-producing.

Central question

Can the idea of a self-producing world be stated rigorously enough to study, rather than just gestured at?

Why this isn't new, and where the gap is

The space of "why does anything exist" is heavily worked. A few landmarks worth knowing before proposing anything:

  • Tegmark's Mathematical Universe Hypothesis — every consistent mathematical structure exists; physical existence just is membership in such a structure.
  • Derek Parfit's work on "selectors" — he asked precisely what could pick the actual reality out of the possible ones, and catalogued candidate selectors (fullness, simplicity, goodness) without being able to justify choosing one.
  • David Lewis's modal realism — all possible worlds are equally real.

What these share is a selection problem: if many self-consistent realities are possible, what distinguishes the actual one? The proposal here doesn't claim to solve that. It claims something narrower and, I think, more tractable: that the "self-producing loop" intuition can be expressed as a fixed-point condition, and that doing so turns a vague metaphor into a question with mathematical structure.

The formalization

A fixed point of a function f is a value that maps to itself:

f(x) = x

You put x in, you get the same x back. This is exactly the shape of a self-producing structure — something defined through itself. So the proposal is:

Let X be a space of possible worlds. Let f: X → X be a generating operator — given a candidate world, f returns the world that candidate's own laws and contents entail. Then a self-producing world is a fixed point of f: a world w with f(w) = w, one that generates exactly itself.

Two things are worth making explicit, because they're easy to get wrong.

f is a generator, not a test. It's tempting to define f as "ask whether a world is self-producing" — a yes/no predicate. But a predicate outputs a truth value, not a world, so f(x) = x becomes meaningless. For the fixed-point framing to work, f must take a world and return a world: "the world this one produces." Being self-producing is then not something f checks — it is the property of being a fixed point of f.

f is not simply "the laws of physics stepping time forward." If f advances a world by one moment, its fixed points are static states that never change — frozen universes — which isn't the idea. The loop is about a whole history/structure generating itself, so f has to operate on entire worlds as single objects, not on momentary snapshots. This pushes the natural home of the problem toward the logic-and-computation tradition of self-reference (Tarski and Kleene fixed points) rather than only the analysis of dynamical systems.

What fixed-point theory could buy — and what it can't

The reason to reach for this machinery is that mathematics already has theorems guaranteeing fixed points exist under stated conditions. The relevant ones:

  • Banach (contraction mapping): if f "shrinks distances" between worlds on a complete metric space, it has exactly one fixed point. This is the dream target — uniqueness would mean there is only one self-producing world, so the selection problem dissolves.
  • Brouwer: a continuous map on a suitable space has at least one fixed point — existence, but possibly many (the many-worlds branch).
  • Tarski: an order-preserving map on a lattice has a structured set of fixed points. This is the tool if "consistency" is formalized logically rather than via a distance, and it is the mathematics behind recursive self-reference.

To actually invoke Banach, several things must be established in order:

  1. Define X, the space of possible worlds.
  2. Define a metric on X — a genuine notion of distance between two worlds, satisfying the metric axioms.
  3. Prove the space is complete.
  4. Define the generating operator f: X → X.
  5. Prove f is a contraction.

Steps 1–3 set the stage; completeness alone proves nothing about how many fixed points exist. Step 5 is where the real content lives — and where the idea is most likely to fail. There is no a priori reason a generating operator on worlds should shrink differences; generic dynamics expand them (that is what chaos is). So the honest expectation is that f is usually not a contraction, which means uniqueness does not come for free, and multiple self-producing worlds are the default.

That observation is itself the research direction.

The honest ceiling

Even a complete success here has a hard limit, and it should be stated plainly.

A fixed point of f is a world that is mathematically self-consistent. It does not follow that the world is instantiated — that it is actual rather than merely a coherent possibility. The fixed point of the cosine function exists mathematically whether or not anything physical realizes it. So this program, at its very best, could show that a self-producing world is coherent and perhaps unique among the consistent ones. It cannot cross the gap from "consistent" to "actually exists."

That gap is the brute fact. It does not disappear; it relocates — from "why does the universe exist?" to "why does consistency confer existence?" Any framework that explains existence smuggles a brute fact in somewhere. The value of formalizing is not eliminating it but making precisely visible what is being explained (the structure and possibly the uniqueness of the loop) and what is not (its instantiation).

The proposal, stated narrowly

Stripped of grandiosity, here is the actual research question:

What structural property must a generating operator f have for it to possess a unique, non-trivial fixed point — and does anything about physical law plausibly place our universe's "operator" in that class?

This is narrow, attackable, and descends directly from the original question about how a self-causing structure could get off the ground. The first half is mathematics (characterize the operators with unique non-trivial fixed points). The second half is where it would connect — if at all — to physics.

I make no claim that this succeeds. I claim only that it converts a 2,500-year-old intuition into a question with enough structure to be worked on, and that it is honest about exactly where it stops.

Reading list

  • Keith Conrad — The Contraction Mapping Theorem (free expository notes) — the cleanest on-ramp to Banach.
  • Tarski's fixed-point theorem and Kleene's recursion theorem — the self-reference tradition in logic and computer science.
  • Max Tegmark — Our Mathematical Universe — the strongest statement of "consistency = existence."
  • Derek Parfit — "Why Anything? Why This?" — the selector problem, stated by the person who framed it best.
  • David Lewis — On the Plurality of Worlds — modal realism.
